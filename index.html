    00

    Charge, fields & Maxwell

    Everything in a circuit is charge being pushed by a field. Voltage, current, capacitors, inductors and antennas are all special cases of the four equations here.

    Core

    The four circuit quantities

    Charge is the stuff, current is how fast it moves, voltage is the push per unit charge, and power is push times flow.

    currentI = dQ/dt voltage (energy per charge)V = W/Q powerP = V·I
    • Units: coulomb (C), ampere (A = C/s), volt (V = J/C), watt (W = J/s).
    • Voltage is always between two points. "The voltage at a node" quietly means "relative to ground".
    • Conventional current flows from + to −. Electrons drift the other way; the math does not care as long as you stay consistent.
    Examples 3

    USB charger

    A 5 V charger delivering 2 A supplies P = 5 × 2 = 10 W.

    Charge in a battery

    1 A for one hour moves Q = I·t = 1 × 3600 = 3600 C. That is what "1 Ah" means.

    Energy to move a charge

    Moving 2 C through a 9 V difference takes W = Q·V = 18 J.

    Core

    Voltage is the field summed along a path

    Walk from a to b and add up how much the electric field pushes against you. That total is the voltage difference.

    ΔV = Vb − Va = −∫ab E · druniform fieldV = E·d
    • The field points from high potential to low potential, so walking with the field lowers V (hence the minus sign).
    • In a static field the path does not matter. Around any closed loop the sum is 0, which is exactly Kirchhoff's voltage law in Level 01.
    Examples 2

    Parallel plates

    A 1000 V/m field across a 2 mm gap gives V = 1000 × 0.002 = 2 V.

    Breakdown

    Dry air breaks down near 3 MV/m. A 1 mm gap can therefore hold off roughly 3 kV before it sparks.

    Fixed

    Faraday's integral form is ∮ E·dl = −dΦB/dt (the time derivative of flux). The note's "δ∂" was a lost derivative.

    Examples 4

    Gauss: field of a point charge

    Pick a sphere of radius r. E is the same everywhere on it, so E·4πr² = Q/ε₀ and E = Q/(4πε₀r²), which is Coulomb's law.

    Ampère: field around a wire

    10 A in a straight wire, 1 cm away: B = μ₀I/(2πr) = (4π×10⁻⁷ × 10)/(2π × 0.01) = 200 μT, about four times Earth's field.

    Faraday: why transformers need AC

    DC gives a constant flux, so dΦ/dt = 0 and no voltage is induced in the secondary.

    Maxwell's addition: why radio exists

    The μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t term means a changing E makes B, and Faraday says a changing B makes E. Each regenerates the other, so the pair travels on its own at c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). That is the wave antennas launch in Level 12.

    Lab

    Faraday's law in a coil

    A magnet's flux through a coil varies as a sine. Predict: if you double the frequency, what happens to the peak induced voltage?

    Peak EMF = N·2πf·Φ₀
    flux Φ(t) (mWb)induced EMF = −N dΦ/dt (V)

    The EMF peaks where the flux is changing fastest, at its zero crossings, not where the flux is largest. Doubling f doubles the EMF, which is why generators spin fast.

    Recall check

    Why can't a transformer step up a battery's DC voltage?

    Faraday's law induces voltage only from a changing flux. Steady DC makes a constant flux, so dΦ/dt = 0 and the secondary sees nothing after the first instant.

    Which Maxwell term makes electromagnetic waves possible?

    Maxwell's displacement term μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t. Without it a changing E could not create B, and the fields could not sustain each other in empty space.

    01

    Circuit laws

    Three rules solve every resistor circuit: Ohm's law for each part, and Kirchhoff's two laws for how the parts connect.

    Core

    Ohm's law

    Current through a resistor is proportional to the voltage across it.

    V = I·Rpower in a resistorP = I²R = V²/R
    • Resistance R (ohms, Ω) sets how much voltage it takes to push 1 A.
    • Resistors have a power rating (often ¼ W). Exceed it and they overheat.
    Examples 3

    LED current-limiting resistor

    5 V supply, red LED drops 2 V, want 10 mA: R = (5 − 2)/0.010 = 300 Ω. Power: P = 0.01² × 300 = 30 mW, fine for a ¼ W part.

    Heater

    A 24 Ω element on 120 V draws 5 A and dissipates V²/R = 600 W.

    Too-small resistor

    100 Ω across 12 V dissipates 1.44 W. A ¼ W resistor will burn.

    Core

    Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws

    Charge is never lost at a node, and energy is never gained going around a loop.

    KCL, at any nodeΣ Iin = Σ IoutKVL, around any loopΣ V = 0
    • KCL is conservation of charge. KVL is the closed-loop version of −∮E·dl = 0 from Level 00.
    • KVL holds for lumped circuits. A loop that links a changing magnetic flux (Faraday) gains an extra EMF term; that EMF is what we model as an inductor's voltage.
    Fixed

    The name is spelled Kirchhoff, not Kirchoff.

    Examples 2

    KCL at a node

    3 A flows into a node, one branch takes 1 A out. The other branch must carry 2 A out.

    KVL in a loop

    A 9 V battery drives two resistors in series. One drops 4 V, so the other must drop 9 − 4 = 5 V.

    Core

    Series and parallel

    Series parts share a current and add resistance. Parallel parts share a voltage and add conductance.

    seriesR = R₁ + R₂ + …parallel1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …two in parallelR₁∥R₂ = R₁R₂R₁+R₂
    • Parallel result is always smaller than the smallest branch.
    • Two equal resistors in parallel give half of one.
    Examples 2

    Mixed network

    Two 2 kΩ in parallel, then in series with 1 kΩ 2k ∥ 2k = 1 kΩ 1 kΩ + 1 kΩ = 2 kΩ total

    Unequal parallel

    1 kΩ ∥ 4 kΩ = 4/5 kΩ = 800 Ω, smaller than 1 kΩ as expected.

    Context

    Sign conventions

    • Passive sign convention: if current enters the + terminal, the part absorbs P = VI. A negative result means it is supplying power.
    • Ground is just the node you call 0 V. Pick the one with the most connections to save work.

    Recall check

    What is the total resistance of 3 kΩ in parallel with 6 kΩ?

    (3 × 6)/(3 + 6) = 2 kΩ.

    Which conservation law is KCL?

    Conservation of charge: charge cannot pile up at a node.

    02

    Dividers & network analysis

    Combine Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws into shortcuts. The voltage divider is the single most reused circuit in EE, and Thévenin lets you shrink any linear network to two parts.

    Core

    Voltage divider

    Added

    Two resistors in series split the input voltage in proportion to their resistances.

    Vout = Vin · R₂R₁ + R₂with a load RLreplace R₂ by R₂∥RL
    • Derivation: one current I = Vin/(R₁+R₂) flows through both, so Vout = I·R₂.
    • Loading: anything you connect to the output sits in parallel with R₂ and pulls Vout down. Keep RL ≥ 10·R₂ or add an op-amp buffer (Level 08).
    • Dividers set reference voltages and bias points and read resistive sensors. They are a poor power supply because of loading and wasted current.
    Examples 4

    12 V down to 5 V

    Want Vout/Vin = 5/12. Pick R2 = 5 kΩ, R1 = 7 kΩ Vout = 12 × 5/(7+5) = 5.00 V Current = 12/12k = 1 mA (wasted continuously)

    Loading ruins it

    10 V, R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ → Vout = 5 V unloaded Connect RL = 10 kΩ: R2∥RL = 5 kΩ Vout = 10 × 5/(10+5) = 3.33 V (33% error)

    Thermistor sensor

    An NTC thermistor as R₂ (10 kΩ at 25 °C) with a fixed 10 kΩ R₁ gives 2.5 V from 5 V at room temperature. When it warms, R₂ drops and Vout falls; an ADC (Level 05) reads the change.

    Potentiometer

    A pot is a divider whose wiper slides along one resistor, so R₁ + R₂ stays constant while the ratio changes. Volume knobs work this way.

    Current divider

    Added

    Parallel branches split current in inverse proportion to resistance: more current takes the easier path.

    I₁ = I · R₂R₁ + R₂

    Note the opposite resistor is on top, the reverse of the voltage divider.

    Example 1

    Splitting 30 mA

    R₁ = 1 kΩ, R₂ = 2 kΩ: I₁ = 30 × 2/3 = 20 mA, I₂ = 10 mA. The smaller resistor takes twice the current.

    Build

    Node-voltage, mesh, and superposition

    Added

    When a circuit is not simple series and parallel, write KCL at each node (or KVL around each mesh) and solve the equations.

    • Node-voltage: pick ground, name the other node voltages, write KCL at each one using (Va − Vb)/R for each branch current.
    • Mesh: assign a loop current to each window of the circuit, write KVL around each.
    • Superposition (linear circuits only): find the response to each source alone and add them. Turn off a voltage source by shorting it and a current source by opening it.
    Example 1

    One unknown node

    10 V —[2 kΩ]— Va —[2 kΩ]— gnd, plus 1 mA injected into Va KCL at Va: (10 − Va)/2k + 1m = Va/2k → 10 − Va + 2 = Va → Va = 6 V Check by superposition: 5 V (source alone) + 1 V (1 mA × 1 kΩ) = 6 V
    Core

    Thévenin and Norton equivalents

    Seen from any two terminals A and B, a linear circuit behaves exactly like one source plus one resistor.

    Thévenin: source in series with RVTh = Voc Norton: source in parallel with RIN = Isc same R for bothRTh = RN = Voc/Isc
    • Voc: open-circuit voltage across A–B. Isc: current through a wire shorting A–B.
    • Shortcut for RTh when there are no dependent sources: turn off every source (V→short, I→open) and find the resistance looking into A–B.
    • Convert freely: VTh = IN·RTh (source transformation).
    Fixed

    The notes wrote RN = VTh/isc and labelled the Norton current ITh. Norton's current is IN = Isc, and RN equals RTh.

    Examples 2

    Thévenin of a divider

    10 V source, R1 = 6 kΩ (top), R2 = 4 kΩ (bottom), output across R2 Voc = 10 × 4/10 = 4 V → VTh Isc = 10 V / 6 kΩ = 1.667 mA → IN RTh = Voc/Isc = 6k ∥ 4k = 2.4 kΩ

    This is why dividers sag under load: they have a Thévenin resistance of R₁∥R₂ in series with their output.

    Why it matters

    A sensor, battery or amplifier output can be summarised as VTh and RTh (its "output resistance") without knowing what is inside.

    RL = RThPmax = VTh²4RTh

    At that point efficiency is only 50%. Power grids avoid it (they want efficiency); RF links and antennas aim for it (Level 12, impedance matching).

    03

    Capacitors, inductors & transients

    Resistors respond instantly. Capacitors and inductors store energy, so circuits with them take time to settle. This level introduces time; the next level introduces frequency.

    Core

    Capacitor

    Stores charge in an electric field. Its voltage cannot jump instantly.

    Q = C·Vi = C dvdtenergy½CV²
    • Capacitance: charge stored per volt (farads).
    • Resists changes in voltage. A sudden jump would need infinite current.
    • DC steady state: fully charged, no current, acts as an open.
    Core

    Inductor

    Stores energy in a magnetic field. Its current cannot jump instantly.

    λ = L·Iv = L didtenergy½LI²
    • Inductance: flux linkage per amp (henries). The voltage is Faraday's law from Level 00.
    • Resists changes in current.
    • DC steady state: constant current, no voltage, acts as a short.
    Fixed

    The notes listed the inductor as "resists changes in current / resists voltage". The clean pair is: capacitor resists changes in voltage, inductor resists changes in current. Each one passes the other quantity freely at the right moment (a capacitor allows a burst of current; an inductor allows a spike of voltage).

    Core

    First-order transients and the time constant

    Added

    Any circuit with one R and one C (or L) moves exponentially from where it starts to where it will end, with time constant τ.

    x(t) = x∞ + (x₀ − x∞) e−t/τRCτ = RCRLτ = L/R
    • After 1τ: 63% of the way there. After 5τ: over 99%, treat it as done.
    • Recipe: find x₀ (capacitor voltage or inductor current just before the switch), find x∞ (treat C as open, L as short), find τ using the RTh the part sees.
    Examples 3

    Charging a capacitor

    10 kΩ, 100 μF, 5 V step, starts at 0 V τ = 10k × 100μ = 1 s v(1 s) = 5(1 − e⁻¹) = 3.16 V v(5 s) = 4.97 V

    Inductor kickback

    Opening a switch on a relay coil forces di/dt to be huge, so v = L di/dt spikes to hundreds of volts. A flyback diode (Level 07) gives the current a path and clamps the spike.

    Switch-debounce and reset timers

    Microcontroller reset circuits use an RC so the reset pin rises slowly over a few τ after power-up.

    Recall check

    Right after a switch connects an uncharged capacitor to a source, what does the capacitor look like?

    A short circuit. Its voltage is still 0 and cannot jump, so it draws the full V/R current at first.

    Doubling R in an RL circuit does what to τ?

    Halves it, because τ = L/R. In an RC circuit doubling R doubles τ.

    04

    AC, phasors & impedance

    Drive the same parts with a sine wave. Every rule from Levels 01–02 still works if you replace resistance with complex impedance.

    Core

    Sinusoids and phasors

    Added

    A sine at a known frequency is fully described by its amplitude and phase, so we write it as one complex number: a phasor.

    v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ)⟷ V = Vm∠φangular frequencyω = 2πfRMS of a sineVrms = Vm/√2
    Examples 2

    Wall outlet

    US mains is 120 Vrms at 60 Hz, so the peak is 120√2 ≈ 170 V and ω = 377 rad/s.

    Adding phasors

    Two 1 V sines 90° apart add to √2 ∠45° ≈ 1.41 V, not 2 V. Phasors add like vectors.

    Core

    Impedance and reactance

    Impedance Z = V/I as phasors. Its real part is resistance; its imaginary part, reactance, stores and returns energy.

    ZR = RZL = jωLZC = 1jωCreactancesXL = 2πfL, XC = 12πfC
    • Inductor: positive reactance that grows with f, so it blocks high frequencies.
    • Capacitor: negative reactance (−j/ωC) that shrinks with f, so it passes high frequencies and blocks DC.
    • Series, parallel, dividers, Thévenin: all still work with Z in place of R. A filter is just a divider with a capacitor in it (Level 06).
    Fixed

    The notes wrote xc = 1/2πfc and R = jωL. Correct forms: XC = 1/(2πfC) (the whole 2πfC is in the denominator) and ZL = jωL (it is impedance, not resistance).

    Examples 3

    Capacitor at audio frequencies

    1 μF at 1 kHz: XC = 1/(2π·1000·10⁻⁶) ≈ 159 Ω. At 10 kHz it is 15.9 Ω.

    Inductor at radio frequencies

    10 μH at 100 MHz: XL = 2π·10⁸·10⁻⁵ ≈ 6.3 kΩ, a near-open "RF choke".

    Series RC impedance

    R = 100 Ω, XC = 159 Ω at 1 kHz Z = 100 − j159 Ω |Z| = √(100² + 159²) = 188 Ω, angle = −57.8°
    Core

    ELI the ICE man

    In an inductor (L), voltage (E) leads current (I) by 90°. In a capacitor (C), current (I) leads voltage (E) by 90°.

    inductorV = jωL·I → V leads by +90°capacitorI = jωC·V → I leads by +90°
    Fixed

    The notes said each pair is "in phase". They are 90° out of phase; only a resistor keeps V and I in phase. The multiplication by j is what rotates the phasor 90°.

    What happens to a capacitor's impedance as frequency goes to zero?

    05

    Signals & systems

    Step back from individual parts and treat a whole circuit as a system with an input and an output. Transforms turn hard time-domain math into multiplication.

    Core

    LTI systems and convolution

    A linear time-invariant system is completely described by its impulse response h. The output is the input convolved with h.

    y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = Σk x[k] · h[n − k]continuousy(t) = ∫ x(τ) h(t − τ) dτ
    • Read it as: flip h, slide it to position n, multiply point by point with x, add up.
    • Every R, L, C, op-amp circuit you have seen so far is LTI.
    Examples 2

    Moving average

    h = [⅓, ⅓, ⅓] replaces each sample with the mean of it and the two before it. It smooths noise, so it is a simple low-pass filter.

    Echo

    h = [1, 0, 0, 0.5] outputs the input plus a half-volume copy 3 samples later.

    Lab

    Convolution, one step at a time

    Slide n. The flipped h moves across x; the output sample is the sum of the products where they overlap.

    y[n]
    x[k]h[n−k] (flipped, shifted)y[n] so far

    Transfer functions, poles and stability

    Poles are the values of s where H(s) blows up. Their position tells you whether the system's natural response decays or grows.

    • Sampling maps z = esT: the left half-plane folds into the inside of the unit circle.

    Sampling and the Nyquist rate

    To capture a signal without ambiguity, sample faster than twice its highest frequency. Slower than that, high frequencies masquerade as low ones (aliasing).

    fs > 2 fmaxNyquist rate2fmaxNyquist frequencyfs/2aliasfa = |f − k·fs|
    • Nyquist rate belongs to the signal (2fmax). Nyquist frequency belongs to the sampler (fs/2). They get mixed up often.
    • Real systems put an analog low-pass "anti-aliasing" filter (Level 06) before the ADC.
    Examples 2

    CD audio

    Hearing tops out near 20 kHz, so CDs sample at 44.1 kHz, leaving room for the anti-aliasing filter's transition band.

    Wagon-wheel effect

    A wheel spinning at 23 rev/s filmed at 24 frames/s appears to turn backwards at 1 rev/s: |23 − 24| = 1.

    Added

    06

    Filters

    A filter is a frequency-dependent divider. Level 04 gave you the impedances, Level 05 gave you H(s); now shape which frequencies pass.

    Core

    Decibels and Bode plots

    Added

    Filters are described in decibels on a log-frequency axis, because gains multiply and frequency ranges span decades.

    voltage gaindB = 20 log₁₀|Vout/Vin|power gaindB = 10 log₁₀(Pout/Pin)cutoff−3 dB ≈ 0.707 = half power
    • −20 dB = ×0.1 in voltage. −40 dB = ×0.01.
    • Each pole adds −20 dB/decade of roll-off beyond the cutoff. An order-n filter rolls off at 20n dB/decade.
    Core

    First-order RC filter

    Added

    Put R and C in a divider. Take the output across C for low-pass, across R for high-pass.

    AC coupling

    A series capacitor into an amplifier input is a high-pass filter that blocks the DC offset and passes the audio.

    Build

    Filter types and design vocabulary

    • Low-pass: passes frequencies below fc.
    • High-pass: passes frequencies above fc.
    • Band-pass: passes a band between two edges.
    • Notch (band-stop): removes a narrow band, such as 60 Hz hum.
    • Passband: gain ≈ 1. Stopband: gain ≈ 0.
    • Transition band: the slope between them; no real filter is a brick wall.
    • Roll-off: how steep the transition is.
    • Ripple: gain wobbles, e.g. 1 V in gives 1.1 V at one frequency and 0.9 V at another.
    • Phase linearity: all frequencies delayed equally, so pulse shapes survive.

    07

    Semiconductors

    Doping lets us decide where charge carriers are. That gives one-way valves (diodes) and controllable valves (transistors), the parts every amplifier and logic gate is built from.

    Core

    Doping: n-type and p-type

    Pure silicon barely conducts. Adding a few impurity atoms adds free carriers and raises conductivity, whichever type you add.

    n-type: dope with phosphorus (5 valence electrons). The extra electron is free to move. Majority carriers: electrons.

    p-type: dope with boron (3 valence electrons). The missing electron is a "hole" that moves like a positive charge. Majority carriers: holes.

    Fixed

    The notes said boron makes silicon more like an insulator. Boron doping makes p-type silicon more conductive, just through holes instead of electrons. Also, the element is silicon; silicone is a rubbery polymer.

    Core

    PN junction diode

    Added

    Join p and n material and you get a one-way valve: current flows from p (anode) to n (cathode) once the forward voltage exceeds about 0.7 V in silicon.

    I = Is(eV/(nVT) − 1)thermal voltageVT ≈ 26 mV at room temp
    • Carriers near the junction recombine, leaving a depletion region with a built-in field. Forward bias shrinks it; reverse bias widens it and blocks current.
    • Quick model: 0.7 V drop when on, open when off.
    Examples 3

    Rectifier

    Four diodes in a bridge flip the negative half of AC upward, the first step in every wall adapter.

    LED

    A diode whose recombination emits light. Red drops about 2 V, blue about 3 V.

    Flyback diode

    Across a relay coil, reverse-biased in normal operation. When the switch opens, it carries the inductor's current (Level 03) and clamps the voltage spike.

    Build

    BJT: current-controlled current source

    A small base current controls a collector current β times larger.

    IC = β IBVBE ≈ 0.7 VIE = IC + IB
    • Regions: cutoff (off), forward-active (amplifier), saturation (fully on switch, VCE ≈ 0.2 V).

    MOSFET: voltage-controlled current source

    The gate voltage creates a conducting channel. No steady gate current flows, which is why MOSFETs dominate digital chips.

    cutoffVGS < Vth: ID = 0triode (VDS < Vov)ID = k(VovVDS − ½VDS²)saturationID = ½k Vov²

    Here Vov = VGS − Vth and k = μCoxW/L. As a switch, triode is "on" (small resistance). As an amplifier, saturation is where the current depends on VGS, not VDS.

    Op-amps

    An op-amp is a transistor circuit with enormous gain. Wrap it in negative feedback and two simple rules let you design amplifiers, buffers, integrators and active filters.

    Core

    The two golden rules

    Added

    With negative feedback, an ideal op-amp adjusts its output until both inputs are at the same voltage, and no current flows into the inputs.

    rule 1V₊ = V₋rule 2I₊ = I₋ = 0open loopVout = A(V₊ − V₋), A ≈ 10⁵
    • The output can never exceed the supply rails. Ask for more and it clips.
    • Without feedback (comparator), rule 1 does not apply: the output slams to a rail.
    Example: deriving the inverting gain 1
    V₊ is grounded → V₋ = 0 (rule 1) Current in through Rin: (Vin − 0)/Rin No current into V₋ (rule 2), so it all flows through Rf: (Vin − 0)/Rin = (0 − Vout)/Rf → Vout = −(Rf/Rin)·Vin
    Core

    Standard configurations

    Buffer (follower)Vout = VinDraws no input current, so it fixes divider loading from Level 02.

    InvertingVout = −Vin(Rf/Rin)Flips polarity; gain can be below 1. Input impedance = Rin.
    Summing AddedVout = −Rf(V₁/R₁ + V₂/R₂ + …)Audio mixers, simple DACs.

    Why thermal oxide stays thin (the notes asked "Why?"): Deal–Grove growth is reaction-limited (linear in time) for thin oxide, then diffusion-limited (thickness ∝ √time) once oxygen must diffuse through the oxide already grown. Doubling thickness takes roughly four times as long.

    Core

    Lithography

    Light through a mask changes the solubility of a photoresist, so the developer leaves a stencil of the mask on the wafer.

    • Positive resist: exposed regions dissolve. The pattern left matches the mask's opaque areas.
    • Negative resist: exposed regions stay. The pattern left is the inverse.
    • A stepper aligns mask to wafer; MEMS often needs front and back masks.
    • Resist must be thick enough to survive the etch. Selectivity = (etch rate of the film) ÷ (etch rate of the mask). Higher is better.
    • Lift-off: pattern resist first, deposit a thin film over everything, dissolve the resist in acetone so the film on top of it lifts away. No etchant, clean edges; works for thin films only.
    Fixed

    The notes defined selectivity as photoresist rate to material rate. It is usually quoted the other way, film rate ÷ mask rate, so a selectivity of 10 means the film etches 10× faster than the resist.

    Core

    Etching

    Isotropic: etches equally in all directions, undercutting the mask. Usually wet chemistry.

    Anisotropic: etches mainly downward. Aspect ratio AR = vertical ÷ lateral etch. Dry plasma (physical, chemical or both) or crystal-orientation wet etches.

    • Wet etching: dip in a chemical solution. Cheap, selective, usually isotropic.
    • Dry/plasma etching: an RF-driven plasma (with a matching network, Level 12 idea) supplies reactive or bombarding ions. Directional.
    Fixed

    The notes put 54.7° angles under isotropic. The 54.7° sidewall comes from anisotropic wet etching of (100) silicon in KOH, which stops on the slow (111) crystal planes.
