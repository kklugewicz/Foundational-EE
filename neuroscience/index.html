    01

    The neuron

    How does one cell turn inputs into a decision to fire?

    Builds on: nothing. This is the ground floor.
    Core

    A neuron collects signals on its dendrites, adds them up at the axon hillock, and if the total crosses a threshold (≈ −55 mV) it fires an all-or-nothing spike down its axon. Strength is coded by how often it fires, never by how big the spike is.

    Key · anatomyParts, in the order a signal travels added

    1. Dendrites receive inputs from other neurons.
    2. Soma (cell body) keeps the cell alive and pools the inputs.
    3. Axon hillock is the decision point where the threshold is tested.
    4. Axon, often wrapped in myelin, carries the spike away.
    5. Axon terminals release chemicals onto the next cell (Level 2).

    Key · the batteryResting potential ≈ −70 mV added

    The inside of the cell sits negative relative to outside. Two things hold it there:

    • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump pushes 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
    • K⁺ leak channels let positive K⁺ drift out.

    Result: lots of Na⁺ waiting outside, ready to rush in when gates open.

    Key · the spikeAction potential, step by step added

    1. Threshold: inputs push the hillock from −70 to about −55 mV.
    2. Rising phase: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ floods in, voltage shoots to about +40 mV.
    3. Falling phase: Na⁺ channels shut (inactivate) and slower K⁺ channels open, K⁺ flows out.
    4. Undershoot: K⁺ channels close late, so voltage dips below rest.
    5. Refractory period: for ~1–2 ms another spike is impossible, then harder than usual. This keeps spikes moving one way and caps the firing rate.

    Fire a neuron

    Interactive · Hodgkin–Huxley model

    This is the real 1952 squid-axon model that won a Nobel Prize. Inject a pulse of current and watch the voltage. Try a pulse that is too weak, then just strong enough. Then add a second pulse and shrink the gap until it fails: that is the refractory period.

    Spikes fired: 0Peak voltage: 0━ voltage━ Na⁺ conductance━ K⁺ conductance

    Example rate coding · Touching a warm mug vs. a hot pan

    A heat-sensing neuron in your fingertip fires every spike at the same size. A warm mug might drive it at 10 spikes per second; a hot pan at 200. Your brain reads intensity from the rate. You saw the same idea in your notes about the retina: light in the right spot "increases signal frequency".

    DetailMyelin makes spikes fast

    Myelin insulates the axon, so the spike jumps between gaps (nodes of Ranvier). This is saltatory conduction: up to ~120 m/s, versus ~1 m/s bare. In multiple sclerosis the immune system strips myelin and signals slow or fail. Practice also adds myelin to heavily used pathways.

    DetailGlia, the support crew

    Astrocytes feed neurons and clean up transmitter. Oligodendrocytes (brain) and Schwann cells (body) make myelin. Microglia are immune cells that also prune unused synapses.

    Check yourself

    Why can't a stronger stimulus make a bigger spike?

    Once threshold is crossed, the Na⁺ channels open in a self-reinforcing burst that always runs to the same peak. Intensity is carried by spike rate instead.

    Which ion causes the rising phase, and which the falling phase?

    Na⁺ rushing in raises the voltage; K⁺ flowing out brings it back down.

    What would happen with no refractory period?

    A spike could re-trigger the patch of axon behind it and travel backward, and firing rates would have no ceiling.

    02

    Synapses & signaling

    How does one neuron change what another neuron does?

    Builds on: 01 The neuron
    Core

    At a synapse, a spike releases neurotransmitter that nudges the next cell's voltage up (excitatory) or down (inhibitory). Each neuron is constantly summing thousands of nudges and only fires when excitation wins by enough.

    Key · the handoffChemical transmission in 5 steps added

    1. Spike reaches the axon terminal.
    2. Ca²⁺ rushes in.
    3. Vesicles fuse and dump transmitter into the cleft.
    4. Transmitter binds receptors on the next cell, opening ion channels.
    5. It is cleared by reuptake or enzymes, so the signal is brief.

    Key · summationAdding up inputs added

    • EPSP: a small push toward threshold (usually glutamate).
    • IPSP: a small push away from it (usually GABA).
    • Temporal summation: inputs arriving close together in time stack before they fade.
    • Spatial summation: inputs from many synapses at once stack.

    Example drugs at the synapse · How SSRIs and L-DOPA work

    SSRIs (like fluoxetine) block serotonin reuptake (step 5), so serotonin lingers in the cleft longer.

    Parkinson's disease kills dopamine neurons that feed the basal ganglia, so people struggle to start movements. L-DOPA is a building block the surviving cells turn into extra dopamine.

    Example inhibition matters · Why seizures happen

    When inhibition fails (low GABA activity), excitation spreads unchecked and huge populations fire together: a seizure. Many anti-seizure drugs and benzodiazepines work by boosting GABA. Healthy thinking depends on a careful balance of excitation and inhibition.

    Check yourself

    Why did one excitatory click fail but three quick ones work?

    Each EPSP leaks away within tens of milliseconds. Arriving close together, they stack (temporal summation) past −55 mV.

    What is the difference between an EPSP and an action potential?

    An EPSP is small, graded, and fades with distance and time. An action potential is all-or-nothing and travels the full length of the axon.

    03

    Learning in circuits

    If neurons just sum and fire, where does learning live?

    Builds on: 02 Synapses
    Core

    Learning is changing synapse strengths. "Neurons that fire together wire together": connections that help a cell fire get stronger, unused ones weaken and get pruned. Two learning systems sit on top of this: model-free (repeat what got rewarded) and model-based (simulate first, then choose).

    Key · the mechanismLTP and the NMDA receptor added

    Long-term potentiation (LTP): repeatedly activating a synapse makes it stronger for hours to years.

    The NMDA receptor is a coincidence detector. It opens only when (1) glutamate is bound and (2) the receiving cell is already depolarized. Then Ca²⁺ enters and the cell adds more receptors to that synapse. So it only strengthens connections that were active when the cell fired.

    LTD (long-term depression) is the reverse and weakens synapses.

    Key · use it or lose itPractice reshapes the cortex your notes

    Learning a skill well can grow the region of cortex that does it.

    • Children overproduce synapses, then prune the unused ones.
    • Heavily used paths gain myelin and get faster.
    • Unused territory gets taken over by neighbors.
    Example · Taxi drivers and violinists

    London taxi drivers who memorized the city's 25,000 streets had a larger posterior hippocampus, and it grew with years on the job. String players have an enlarged cortical map for the fingers of the left hand, the hand that does the fine fingering.

    Key · two ways to learnModel-free vs. model-based your notes

    Model-free (habit)Model-based (simulation)
    RuleRepeat what was rewarded beforeImagine outcomes, pick the best
    SpeedFast, automaticSlow, effortful
    FlexibilityPoor; keeps going when the world changesHigh; adapts instantly to new info
    BrainBasal ganglia → motor cortexPrefrontal cortex + hippocampus + sensory cortex
    Covered inLevel 8 habitsLevel 8 vicarious trial and error

    Keep this table in mind. Most of the rest of the page is about how these two systems are built and how one trains the other.

    Example both systems · Learning to drive

    Week one: every lane change is model-based. You picture the mirror, the gap, the car behind, and you are exhausted after 20 minutes. Year five: lane changes are model-free and you hold a conversation while doing them. The same synapse-strengthening rule drove both, in different circuits.

    DetailCritical periods

    Some circuits only wire properly during a window early in life. A kitten with one eye covered for its first months never develops normal vision in that eye, even after the cover is removed. Your notes mention a related case: the aPFC loses its layer 4 in the first 6–12 months (see Level 8).

    Check yourself

    Why does the NMDA receptor need two conditions?

    So a synapse strengthens only when its input actually helped the cell fire. Input alone, or firing alone, is not enough. That is "fire together, wire together" in hardware.

    Which system is in charge when you drive home on autopilot?

    Model-free. The basal ganglia run a well-rewarded sequence without consulting a simulation.

    04

    The brain map

    Where do billions of these circuits live, and what does each region do?

    Builds on: 03 Learning · zooms out from cells to regions
    Core

    The cortex (outer sheet, four lobes per side) builds the model of the world. Deep structures filter input (thalamus), tag what matters (amygdala, hypothalamus), store new memories (hippocampus) and run automatic actions (basal ganglia). The cerebellum and brainstem handle coordination and survival.

    Key · deep structuresUnder the cortex your notes

    • Thalamus: the gateway that filters sensory data before it reaches cortex. Every sense except smell passes through.
    • Hypothalamus: body state and drives (hunger, thirst, temperature, sleep, hormones).
    • Amygdala: fear, aggression, emotional importance.
    • Hippocampus: turns short-term experiences into long-term memories.
    • Cingulate gyrus: regulates emotion and pain, predicts bad outcomes.
    • Basal ganglia: the automatic, model-free center for goals and execution.

    Memory hook: LGN = Light, MGN = Music.

    Correction to your notes: the hypothalamus is mainly about keeping the body in balance (homeostasis) and producing the bodily side of emotions, like a racing heart. Predicting and regulating emotion is more the job of the cingulate and prefrontal cortex.

    DetailCrossed wiring added

    Each hemisphere senses and moves the opposite side of the body. A stroke in the left motor cortex weakens the right arm. The corpus callosum, a thick bundle of ~200 million axons, links the two halves.

    DetailCerebellum and brainstem added

    The cerebellum holds about 80% of the brain's neurons and fine-tunes timing and coordination. The brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla) runs breathing, heart rate and wakefulness, and its midbrain holds the dopamine neurons from Level 7.

    Example maps inside the map · The homunculus

    The motor strip and the touch strip each hold a map of the body, but scaled by how much control or sensitivity each part needs. Lips, tongue and hands take up huge areas; the back and legs get very little. That is why a paper cut on a fingertip feels worse than one on your shin.

    Check yourself

    Which sense skips the thalamus?

    Smell. It goes directly to olfactory cortex, one reason smells trigger memories and emotions so quickly.

    Damage to the left occipital lobe affects which part of what you see?

    The right half of your visual field in both eyes, because of crossed wiring.

    05

    The neocortex

    What is the repeating unit that builds the model of the world?

    Builds on: 04 Brain map 03 Learning
    Core

    About 90% of cortex is neocortex, a sheet tiled with columns that all share the same six-layer circuit. Each column seems to run one algorithm: predict its input. Because the circuit is the same everywhere, a region's job depends mostly on what input it receives.

    Example same circuit everywhere · Ferrets that see with their hearing cortex

    In 2000, researchers rerouted the eye's input in newborn ferrets so it went to auditory cortex instead of visual cortex. The auditory cortex grew line-detecting cells like V1 and the ferrets used it to see. Sensory-substitution devices do something similar for people: blind users of a tongue display that turns camera images into tingles learn to "see" shapes, and their visual cortex becomes active.

    2/3Layer 4Other cortical areas; layer 5Combines features, talks to cortex

    Upgrade to your notes: layer 1 has few cell bodies, but it matters. It is where feedback from higher regions lands on the tips of L2/3 and L5 dendrites, which is how predictions from "above" shape what a column reports.

    Key · a control loopThalamus ⇄ cortex

    Layer 4 takes input from the thalamus, and layer 6 sends output back to it. That loop lets the cortex adjust what the thalamus passes along, turning some channels up and others down. It is a strong candidate for how attention works.

    DetailGranular vs. agranular your notes

    "Granular" cortex has a visible layer 4, packed with small granule cells. Motor cortex and the agranular prefrontal cortex (aPFC) lack it. That fits their role: they mostly produce output rather than take raw sensory input.

    Check yourself

    A column's only sensory input arrives in which layer, from where?

    Layer 4, from the thalamus.

    Which layer would you damage to stop cortex from driving the basal ganglia?

    Layer 5, the main output to subcortical structures.

    What does the ferret experiment suggest about cortex?

    The circuit is general purpose. Its function comes from its input, so regions differ by wiring, not by kind.

    06

    Seeing: the cortex in action

    How do columns turn spots of light into a recognized object?

    Builds on: 05 Neocortex 02 Inhibition
    Core

    Vision is built in stages, each combining the simpler features below it: spots → lines → contours → shapes and motion → objects and color. Then it splits: the ventral stream to the temporal lobe asks "what is it?", the dorsal stream to the parietal lobe asks "where is it, and how do I reach it?"

    1. RetinaCenter-surround spot detectors
    2. LGNThalamic relay, gain set by L6
    3. V1Lines and edges at one angle
    4. V2Contours, patterns, illusory edges
    5. V3 · MTShape in motion · direction and speed
    6. V4Color, complex shape
    7. StreamsVentral: what · Dorsal: where

    Stage 1: a retinal ganglion cell

    Interactive · center-surround receptive field

    Shine a spot of light on the cell's receptive field and grow it. Light on the center excites; light on the surround suppresses. The best response comes from a spot that exactly fills the center.

    Firing rate: Baseline: 20 spikes/s

    Refinement to your notes: the LGN still responds to spots, not lines. Lines first appear in V1, where a simple cell lines up several LGN spot-detectors along one angle, as in the model below.

    Stage 2: V1 orientation columns

    Interactive · simple cells and inhibition

    Rotate the bar. Each of the 8 simple cells prefers one angle. Turn on lateral inhibition to see how "excite cells of this angle and inhibit cells of other angles" sharpens the answer.

    Key · inside V1's layersYour visual cortex example your notes

    1. Retina → LGN → layer 4, where simple cells build a line at one angle.
    2. Layer 2/3 complex cells respond to the same angle anywhere in a small patch.
    3. Layer 5 mixes in input from other cortical areas (via dendrites in L1) and sends output to the basal ganglia.
    4. Layer 6 sets the gain on the thalamus, controlling firing rates.

    Key · two streamsWhat vs. where your notes

    • Ventral (lower) stream → temporal lobe. Identifies objects, faces, words.
    • Dorsal (upper) stream → parietal lobe. Tracks location and motion and guides your hand.

    You need both to catch a ball: ventral says "ball", dorsal says "there, moving left, fast".

    Example ventral damage · Face blindness

    People with prosopagnosia can see a face clearly, describe its nose and eyes, but cannot tell whose face it is, sometimes even their own. The damage sits in the fusiform face area along the ventral stream.

    Example MT damage · Seeing the world as snapshots

    A patient known as L.M. lost area MT on both sides. Pouring tea looked like a frozen column of liquid; cars seemed to jump from far away to right in front of her. Shape and color were fine. Motion was simply gone.

    Example V2 at work · The Kanizsa triangle

    Three Pac-Man shapes facing inward make you see a bright white triangle that is not drawn. V2 cells fire for these "illusory contours", showing that the visual system fills in its best prediction rather than just reporting pixels.

    Check yourself

    Why does a huge spot of light barely excite an ON-center cell?

    It covers the surround too, and surround inhibition cancels most of the center's excitation. These cells report contrast, not brightness.

    How does a V1 simple cell build an orientation detector?

    It receives input from several LGN spot-detectors whose centers lie along a line at one angle.

    Someone can name a mug but cannot reach for it accurately. Which stream is damaged?

    The dorsal stream.

    07

    Feeling & valuing

    Before planning, how does the brain know what matters?

    Builds on: 04 Deep structures 03 Learning
    Core

    Emotional systems tag situations as good or bad. Dopamine neurons broadcast the reward prediction error: what you got minus what you expected. That error is the brain's main teaching signal for the basal ganglia, and the reason surprise drives learning.

    Key · amygdalaThreat and salience your notes

    Processes fear and aggression. It reacts in about 1/10 of a second, faster than conscious recognition, via a shortcut from the thalamus. It also tags memories as important, which is why emotional events are remembered so vividly.

    Example · The stick that looks like a snake

    You jump back before you know what you saw. The thalamus-to-amygdala shortcut triggered the startle; a moment later the slower cortical route reports "just a stick" and the prefrontal cortex calms the amygdala down.

    Key · cingulate & hypothalamusAlarm and body your notes

    Cingulate gyrus: regulates emotion and pain, notices errors and conflicts, and helps predict negative outcomes. It handles the unpleasantness of pain rather than its location.

    Hypothalamus: turns emotion into body changes (heart rate, stress hormones) and drives hunger, thirst, temperature and sleep.

    Train a dopamine neuron

    Interactive · temporal-difference learning

    Key · the ruleThree cases of prediction error added

    What happensDopamineWhat gets learned
    Reward better than expectedBurst ▲Do more of what led here
    Reward exactly as expectedNo changeNothing new

    Wolfram Schultz recorded this in monkeys in the 1990s. The same equation drives reinforcement-learning algorithms in AI.

    Key · the reward seekerFront of the basal ganglia your notes

    The front (ventral) part of the basal ganglia, centered on the nucleus accumbens, drives the automatic pursuit of rewards. Dopamine prediction errors train it.

    Example · Addiction hijacks the teaching signal

    Drugs like cocaine raise dopamine directly, so the brain receives a "better than expected!" error every time, no matter how often it has been used. Cues linked to the drug pick up enormous value, and craving follows.

    Your notes say lesioning the front basal ganglia completely eradicates craving. The surgical studies (nucleus accumbens ablation, mostly in China in the 2000s) reported strong reductions in craving and relapse for many patients, but results were mixed and the procedure is controversial because of side effects on motivation and pleasure.

    Check yourself

    Why does a fully expected reward produce no dopamine burst?

    Dopamine reports the error, and the error is zero. Nothing needs to be learned.

    Why does the burst move to the cue?

    The cue is now the earliest reliable sign of reward, and its arrival is still unpredictable. So the surprise, and the error, happens at the cue.

    08

    Planning & acting

    How does the brain pick an action, and how does practice make it automatic?

    Builds on: 05 Neocortex 07 Value 03 Two systems
    Core

    If sensory cortex runs the simulation of the world, the frontal lobe controls it: it chooses what to imagine and when, compares imagined outcomes, and sends the winner down a motor hierarchy. With practice, the basal ganglia learn the move and trigger motor cortex directly. That is a habit.

    Key · frontal regionsThree parts of the frontal neocortex your notes

    RegionInputsJob
    aPFC agranular prefrontalAmygdala, hippocampus, basal gangliaHolds the goal, runs simulations toward it, tracks progress. Lacks layer 4.
    gPFC granular prefrontalMostly other cortex, including aPFCModels your own intentions and other people's minds; working memory and abstract reasoning added
    PremotorPFCSimulates mid-level sub-goals and sequences
    Motor cortexPremotor, cerebellum (via thalamus)Simulates then executes specific movements; trains the basal ganglia

    Key · the hierarchyGoal → sub-goal → muscle your notes

    1. aPFC: why. "Lay out the idea I'm writing."
    2. Premotor: what sequence. "These words, this sentence."
    3. Motor cortex: which muscles. "Left index finger, down, now."
    Example · Typing these notes (from your notes)

    While typing, the aPFC holds the high-level goal of conceptually laying out what you are writing. The premotor cortex simulates the specific words and sentence. The motor cortex coordinates each keystroke. Practiced words like "the" are fired off by the basal ganglia without the premotor cortex spelling them out.

    Key · imagination as a toolVicarious trial and error your notes

    By driving simulations in sensory cortex, the frontal lobe can try actions in imagination, predict the outcomes, and only then commit. The same circuit can restrain behavior by holding back the motor cortex.

    Example · A rat at a fork in a maze

    Rats pause at a junction and look left, then right, before choosing. During that pause, hippocampal place cells fire in sequences that sweep down each path ahead, as if the rat is mentally walking it. Damage the frontal cortex and the pausing, and the good choices, drop off.

    From goal-directed to habit

    Interactive · practice slider

    Drag practice from zero to hundreds of repetitions. Watch control shift from the slow frontal route to the fast basal ganglia shortcut, and what you gain and lose along the way.

    Key · how habits formThe frontal lobe trains the basal ganglia your notes

    aPFC, premotor and motor cortex repeatedly run a movement. Dopamine errors stamp it into the basal ganglia. Once fluent, the back (dorsal) basal ganglia activate motor cortex directly. That is model-free reinforcement: efficient, but blind to changes in the goal.

    Example · Driving to your old address

    You moved last month, but after a distracted day you find yourself parked at the old apartment. The aPFC was busy elsewhere, so the basal ganglia ran the well-worn route.

    Key · the brakeInhibitory control added

    The prefrontal cortex can veto a habit before it reaches the muscles. It is slow and tires easily, which is why habits win when you are stressed, rushed or exhausted.

    Example · The Stroop test

    Name the ink color of the word RED. Reading is a habit, so the word fights the answer. The pause you feel is the prefrontal cortex overriding the basal ganglia.

    DetailThe cerebellum predicts movement added

    Before you move, the cerebellum predicts what the movement will feel like and corrects errors mid-motion. This is why you can't tickle yourself: the prediction cancels the sensation. Damage causes clumsy, overshooting movements.

    Detail · hypothesisWhy the aPFC loses layer 4 your notes

    At birth the aPFC has a layer 4, which receives basal ganglia input (via thalamus) that is likely emotional and reward-based. It atrophies in the first 6–12 months. One idea is that those months build an internal model, a sense of self, that the aPFC later acts on. Treat this as a hypothesis, not settled science.

    Example frontal damage · Phineas Gage

    In 1848 an iron rod passed through railroad foreman Phineas Gage's prefrontal cortex. He survived, could walk and talk, but became impulsive and unable to stick to plans. Movement was intact; the controller that chooses and restrains was damaged.

    Check yourself

    Which structure triggers motor cortex for a well-learned movement?

    The basal ganglia, bypassing the aPFC and premotor planning.

    Why are habits hard to change when the goal changes?

    Model-free learning stores "this action was rewarded", not why. Updating needs new experience, while model-based control can adapt by simulating.

    What do the premotor and motor cortex each simulate?

    Premotor: mid-level sub-goals and sequences. Motor: the specific movement it will then execute.

    09

    Memory & navigation

    How does today's experience become something you can recall and plan with?

    Builds on: 03 LTP 08 Simulation
    Core

    The hippocampus turns experiences into long-term memories and uses the same machinery to map space. The entorhinal cortex supplies a general coordinate system (grid and object-vector cells); the hippocampus binds it into specific places and landmarks.

    Key · kinds of memoryNot one system but several added

    • Declarative (you can say it): episodic events and semantic facts. Hippocampus, then cortex.
    • Procedural (skills): basal ganglia and cerebellum.
    • Emotional conditioning: amygdala.
    • Working memory (~4 items for seconds): prefrontal cortex.

    Key · consolidationHippocampus → cortex your notes

    New memories depend on the hippocampus at first. Over weeks to years, much of the memory moves into the cortex. A lot of this happens during sleep, when the hippocampus replays the day's patterns.

    Example · Patient H.M.

    In 1953 Henry Molaison had both hippocampi removed to stop seizures. Afterward he could not form new declarative memories, yet older memories survived and he could still learn new motor skills like mirror drawing, without remembering the practice. One case showed memory is not one system.

    Check yourself

    Grid cell or place cell: which works in any room you enter?

    The grid cell. It provides a general coordinate frame. Place cells remap to new spots in each new environment.

    H.M. could learn mirror drawing but not remember practicing. Why?

    Procedural learning runs through the basal ganglia and cerebellum, which were intact. Remembering the practice session is episodic and needed the hippocampus.

    10

    Language

    How do hearing and movement combine into speech?

    Builds on: 04 Lobes 08 Motor hierarchy 06 Perception
    Core

    Language uses a comprehension area in the temporal lobe (Wernicke's) and a production area at the back of the frontal lobe (Broca's), joined by a fiber bundle, the arcuate fasciculus. Comprehension is built on hearing; production is built on the motor hierarchy.

    1. Ear → MGNThalamic relay for sound
    2. Auditory cortexTemporal lobe; pitch and phonemes
    3. Wernicke'sMeaning of words
    4. Arcuate fasciculusCarries words forward
    5. Broca'sPlans the sentence and its sounds
    6. Motor cortexLips, tongue, larynx

    Key · productionBroca's area your notes

    Sits between the motor cortex and the prefrontal cortex, which matches its role: it is the "premotor cortex" for speech, turning ideas into word sequences.

    Example · Broca's aphasia

    Speech is slow, effortful and telegraphic ("Walk... dog... park"), but comprehension is mostly fine. Paul Broca's famous 1861 patient could only say "tan". People with this aphasia know what they want to say and are frustrated they can't get it out.

    Key · comprehensionWernicke's area your notes

    Sits in the temporal lobe beside auditory cortex, matching its role: it links sounds to meaning.

    Example · Wernicke's aphasia

    Speech is fluent, with normal rhythm, but filled with wrong or invented words ("I called my mother on the television and she didn't understand the door"). Comprehension is poor and the person often doesn't notice anything is wrong.

    DetailLateralization and reading added

    About 95% of right-handers (and most left-handers) have language in the left hemisphere. Reading adds a visual route: V1 → ventral stream ("visual word form area") → angular gyrus → Wernicke's. Damage to the arcuate fasciculus causes conduction aphasia: comprehension and fluent speech, but trouble repeating a phrase back.

    Putting it together

    Can you follow one ordinary moment through every level?

    Builds on: everything above
    Core

    Typing one sentence uses the whole loop: value sets a goal, the frontal lobe simulates and acts, perception checks the result, an error triggers learning, and memory stores it.

    1. 1
      You decide to write a note. The aPFC holds the goal, fed by the hippocampus (what you learned today) and the basal ganglia (the value of finishing). L8 · L9 · L7
    2. 2
      You compose the sentence. Broca's area and premotor cortex simulate the words before any finger moves. L10 · L8
    3. 3
      Your fingers move. Motor cortex fires, layer 5 neurons drive the spinal cord, and the cerebellum smooths timing. Common words run as basal ganglia habits. L8 · L5
    4. 4
      Each keystroke is a chain of spikes. Thousands of EPSPs sum to threshold in each motor neuron; acetylcholine contracts each muscle. L1 · L2
    5. 5
      You see the screen. Retina → LGN → V1 lines → V2 contours → V4 shapes → ventral stream recognizes letters and words. L6
    6. 6
      You spot a typo. Prediction ("the") and input ("teh") don't match. The cingulate flags the error. L7 · L0
    7. 7
      You fix it and learn. The prefrontal brake slows the habit, a correction is simulated and executed, and synapses that caused the slip are adjusted. L3 · L8
    8. 8
      That night, you sleep. The hippocampus replays the session and moves it toward long-term storage in cortex. L9
